Why Your Sales Numbers Never Match Across Walmart, Amazon and Target

By Geetha Avadhanula · November 15, 2026

If you sell in more than one place, you have seen this. Walmart says one number. Amazon says another. Target says a third. Your own spreadsheet says a fourth. And none of them match.

Most teams assume someone made a mistake. Usually nobody did. Each channel is counting something slightly different, and calling it by the same name: sales.

The fix is not a bigger spreadsheet. It is knowing what each number actually counts, and then deciding which number to trust for which question.

Start with one question: what are you comparing?

Before you chase a gap, write down what you are comparing. Same product? Same week? Same definition of sales?

Pick one product and one week, and compare two channels only. If the gap is still there at that level, you have something real to investigate. If it disappears, the problem was in how the numbers were added up, not in the business.

Three things to pin down first:

  • Which product, exactly? One UPC, one size, one pack. Bundles and variety packs hide a lot of mismatch.
  • Which week? A Walmart week, a calendar week and an Amazon reporting week do not always line up.
  • Which sales? Gross sales, net sales after returns, sales after fees and deductions. These are three different numbers.

1. Timing is different in every channel

This is the most common cause, and the easiest to miss.

Walmart reports by its own retail week. Amazon reports by order date, and an order placed on Sunday can ship on Tuesday. Target reports by its own fiscal calendar. Your distributor reports by ship date. Your own site reports by the day the payment cleared.

So the same sale can land in different weeks in different reports. Over a quarter it mostly evens out. In any single week it looks like a gap.

What to do: agree on one week definition for your own reporting, and note it on the report. When you compare channels, compare the same weeks, not the same report dates.

2. Returns are counted differently

Amazon counts an order when it is placed. A return a week later reduces a different week, or shows up as a separate line. Walmart register sales are sales at the till, and a return is a separate event. Your own site may count only completed, non-refunded orders.

If returns are high in a week, gross sales will look fine while net sales fall. Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions.

What to do: always compare net sales with net sales. If one channel gives you gross and another gives you net, convert one of them before you compare.

3. Fees, deductions and chargebacks sit in different places

Amazon takes its fees before you see the money. Walmart takes deductions after the sale, sometimes weeks later, and each one has its own reason code. Target has its own set of fees. A distributor invoice is not register sales at all.

So the sales number and the money number are never the same. A channel can show strong sales and weak cash in the same month, and both reports are right.

What to do: keep sales and cash apart. Track gross sales, deductions and fees as three separate lines. Never compare one channel's gross sales with another channel's net payment.

4. Distributor shipments are not register sales

This one catches a lot of brands. A distributor report shows what left the distributor warehouse. That is a shipment, not a sale to a shopper.

If a distributor shipped a lot in March and stores sold it slowly through April and May, the distributor number will look high in March and the store number will look low. Both are true. They measure different steps.

What to do: label distributor numbers as shipments, never as sales. Use them to check supply, not demand. Demand is what the register or the marketplace recorded.

5. The same product has different names in each channel

Walmart knows your product by UPC. Amazon knows it by ASIN. Target knows it by TCIN. Your own site knows it by SKU. If the mapping between those is wrong, or a bundle shares a code with a single item, sales get counted under the wrong product.

This is the quietest cause of mismatch, and often the biggest. A single wrong mapping can move thousands of dollars between products every week.

What to do: keep one product map, with UPC, ASIN, TCIN and your own SKU on the same row. Check it whenever a new product, size or bundle launches. That check takes an hour and saves months of wrong numbers.

6. Online and store sales are not the same thing

Walmart.com and Walmart stores are two different businesses inside one report. Amazon first party and third party sales are different too. A promotion can move sales from one to the other without changing the total at all.

If you only look at the total, you will miss the move. If you only look at stores, you will think sales fell when they just moved online.

What to do: split online and store sales in every channel, every week. Then add them up. The total is the same, but now you can see where the sale actually happened.

A simple check you can run this week

You do not need new software for this. Pick one product, one week, and two channels.

  • Write down the units and the dollars for each channel, side by side.
  • Note the week definition each channel used.
  • Note whether each number is gross or net of returns.
  • Note the fees and deductions each channel took.
  • Check the product mapping for that one product.

Most of the gap will be explained by those five lines. Whatever is left is the real gap, and that is the one worth investigating.

Which number should you trust?

There is no single true number. There are three numbers, and each one answers a different question.

  • Register and marketplace sales tell you what shoppers bought. Use this for demand, velocity and what to make next.
  • Shipments tell you what left a warehouse. Use this for supply, inventory and whether stores will run out.
  • Net sales after deductions and fees tell you what you actually kept. Use this for money, margins and whether a channel is worth the cost.

Keep all three, label them clearly, and stop expecting them to match. They are not supposed to.

What to fix first

If you fix three things, most of the confusion goes away.

First, one product map. Every UPC, ASIN, TCIN and SKU on one row. Check it at every launch.

Second, one week definition. Pick it, write it down, use it everywhere you compare channels.

Third, one definition of net sales. Gross sales, less returns, less deductions and fees. Use the same definition in every channel.

After that, the remaining gaps are usually real business problems: a stockout, a price change, a promotion that moved sales between channels. Those are the gaps worth your time.
